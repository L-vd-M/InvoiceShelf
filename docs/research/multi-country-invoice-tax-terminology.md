# Multi-Country Invoice Tax Terminology — Should the Tax Label Ever Depend on Country?

Research date: 2026-09-23. This file extends three prior South-Africa-only research rounds in this folder (read first — this file assumes their findings as established fact and does not re-litigate them):

- `docs/research/sa-vat-non-vendor-invoicing.md` — VAT Act 89 of 1991 s.58 makes it a criminal offence for a non-VAT-registered SA business to declare/charge a tax amount; s.20 restricts "tax invoice"/"VAT invoice" wording to registered vendors.
- `docs/research/sa-invoice-tax-vs-vat-labeling.md` — VAT Act s.1 defines "tax" as a synonym for VAT; SARS's own plain-English material always writes "VAT" for real amounts, never generic "Tax"; VAT is SA's only transaction-based consumption tax ever itemised on a sales invoice.
- `docs/research/vat-vs-tax-invoice-terminology.md` — the general conceptual difference between "VAT" (a specific multi-stage mechanism) and "Tax" (a jurisdiction-agnostic placeholder that exists only because most invoicing software serves many countries at once).
- `docs/typst/sa-vat-invoicing-research.typ` — consolidated writeup of the above three, including the code changes actually shipped for the SA-only case (`$isVatRegistered = (bool) $invoice->company->vat_id` gate in the Blade templates).

**Scope of this file**: whether/how the invoice's tax terminology should become *dynamic based on country* — both the issuing company's own country and the customer's country. **This is research only. No code changes were made or are proposed as part of this file. The existing South-Africa-only `vat_id`-gated logic described above stays exactly as it is until the developer reviews this document and decides to act on it.**

## 1. General principle: whose country's law actually governs what appears on the invoice?

This is the foundational design question, because it determines whether "customer's country" changes the tax *rate/applicability* the way "issuer's country" does, or whether it only informs something narrower (a note, a zero-rating flag, a reverse-charge flag).

### 1.1 Domestic sales: origin/supplier-registration based, near-universally

Every VAT/GST system checked for this research (EU, UK, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, India) — and South Africa, per the prior research — works the same way for an ordinary domestic sale: **the supplier's own registration status and country determine what tax regime applies and what may legally be shown on the invoice.** The customer's location is irrelevant to a purely domestic transaction; it is the supplier who is or isn't a registered vendor, who charges (or doesn't), and whose invoice-content rules apply. This is structurally identical to the SA VAT Act s.7(1)(a)/s.20(1) pattern already documented: the charging and invoicing obligations attach to "a supplier, being a registered vendor" — never to the customer's status, for a domestic supply.

Concretely, in every jurisdiction researched: a company registers for VAT/GST *in the country it is trading from* (or has a taxable presence in), and it is that registration — not the customer's location — that determines whether it may issue a document calling itself a "tax invoice"/"VAT invoice" and whether it may show a tax amount at all. This is exactly the same mechanism as SA's s.58/s.20: it is not a coincidence, it is how VAT/GST is built everywhere.

### 1.2 Cross-border/export supplies: this is where the customer's country starts to matter — but only as a switch, not a new rate table

The OECD's **International VAT/GST Guidelines** (adopted as a Council Recommendation, September 2016) are the internationally agreed statement of how cross-border VAT/GST is supposed to work, and they name the mechanism directly: *"the application of the destination principle in VAT achieves neutrality in international trade. Under the destination principle, exports are not subject to tax with refund of input taxes (that is, 'free of VAT' or 'zero-rated') and imports are taxed on the same basis and at the same rates as domestic supplies."* (OECD International VAT/GST Guidelines, 2016 Recommendation.) The Guidelines recommend the destination principle for both B2B and B2C cross-border supplies of goods and services.

Two concrete legal mechanisms implement this, both directly confirmed from the EU VAT Directive's own text (Council Directive 2006/112/EC, consolidated text, confirmed via the retained-EU-law mirror of the same Directive on legislation.gov.uk):

- **Zero-rating exports**: exports of goods leaving the supplier's own VAT territory are typically taxed at 0% by the exporting country (the supplier still charges VAT, just at 0%, and can still reclaim input VAT — this preserves the destination principle, since the importing country then taxes the goods on entry instead). South Africa's own VAT Act does exactly this for exports (s.11, not separately re-researched here since it was outside this file's scope, but consistent with the pattern).
- **Reverse charge**: for many cross-border B2B services, the *supplier* charges no VAT at all, and the *customer* self-accounts for the VAT in their own country instead. This is directly codified in **Article 196 of the EU VAT Directive**: *"VAT shall be payable by any taxable person... to whom the services referred to in Article 44 are supplied, if the services are supplied by a taxable person not established within the territory of the Member State."* (Article 196, confirmed text.) The effect: the invoice from the (foreign) supplier shows no VAT amount at all — often annotated "reverse charge" — and the tax obligation shifts entirely to the recipient's own domestic tax return.

Critically, **Article 203 of the same Directive** establishes a strict, registration-independent liability rule that cuts the other way: *"VAT shall be payable by any person who enters the VAT on an invoice."* (Article 203, confirmed text.) This means that in the EU specifically, if a supplier (registered or not) mistakenly or wrongly puts a VAT amount on an invoice, *that amount becomes legally payable by them regardless of whether they were entitled to charge it* — a different enforcement mechanism from South Africa's criminal-offence model (VAT Act s.58), but a mechanism that arrives at the same practical warning for a template designer: showing a tax amount you weren't entitled to show has real legal consequences, just via a different route (automatic strict-liability debt to the tax authority, in the EU's case, rather than a criminal charge).

### 1.3 Answer to the design question this section exists to settle

**"Customer's country" does not, by itself, change what tax rate or tax label a domestic-registered supplier's invoice should show for a domestic sale.** It only becomes relevant when the *supply itself is cross-border* (export of goods, or a service supplied to a customer in a different country/VAT area than the supplier). Even then, in the two dominant patterns worldwide (zero-rating and reverse charge), the customer's country does not change the *word* used for the tax line — it changes whether an amount appears at all, and may add an explanatory note ("Zero-rated export" / "Reverse charge — VAT to be accounted for by the recipient"). **The issuing company's own country/registration status remains the primary driver of the tax label and whether the block renders at all; the customer's country is, at most, a secondary flag that can suppress the rate or add a note — it should not be treated as a second, independent axis that picks its own label.**

## 2. Reference table of major tax systems worldwide

For each jurisdiction: what the tax is called, whether it is VAT-style (multi-stage, input/output credit) or US-style single-stage sales tax, and — the part that matters most for a template that conditionally shows/hides a tax line — whether that jurisdiction has a restriction analogous to SA VAT Act s.58 (only a registered person may charge/show the tax). Every claim is cited to a primary source; where none could be confirmed within reasonable research time, that is stated explicitly rather than guessed.

### 2.1 United Kingdom — HMRC / VAT Act 1994

VAT is charged and administered under the Value Added Tax Act 1994. The original criminal offence provision for unauthorised invoicing, s.67(1), was **repealed** with effect from 1 April 2010 and replaced by a **civil penalty regime**: Finance Act 2008, Schedule 41, paragraph 2, which defines an "unauthorised person" as anyone who is not registered under the VAT Act (nor a body corporate treated as a group member, nor otherwise specifically authorised), and penalises that person for "the unauthorised issue of an invoice showing VAT" at up to 100% of the potential lost revenue for deliberate and concealed conduct, 70% for deliberate but not concealed, 30% otherwise (HMRC internal manual VCP11264 and the Schedule 41 text itself). This is materially different in *character* from SA's approach — a civil financial penalty rather than a criminal offence carrying imprisonment — but functionally the same design constraint for a template: an unregistered UK business must not show a VAT amount, and the restriction is expressly registration-based, not turnover- or intent-based.

### 2.2 European Union — VAT Directive 2006/112/EC

Treated here as **one representative entry, not all 27 member states**, per the scope given for this research — the Directive harmonizes the VAT *system* (what VAT is, the destination principle for cross-border trade, the reverse-charge mechanism, invoicing content requirements) but each member state administers its own national VAT registration, thresholds, and enforcement. The Directive's own liability rule (Article 203) is a strict-liability payment obligation triggered by the *act* of entering VAT on an invoice — it does not, by itself, criminalize or civilly penalise the act the way UK/SA/India/Australia do; it simply makes the amount owed to the tax authority regardless of whether the issuer was ever entitled to charge it. Whether any *individual* member state layers a UK/SA/India-style penalty or criminal offence on top of Article 203 would require checking that state's own transposing national law — not done here, and explicitly out of scope for a "representative entry."

### 2.3 United States — no federal VAT/GST; sales tax is sub-national

This is structurally the odd one out, and the reason a single "country" setting cannot fully capture US tax treatment even in principle: **there is no federal consumption tax of any kind** — no VAT, no GST, no national sales tax. Sales tax is levied by individual states (45 of the 50 states plus DC have one; a handful, e.g. Oregon, Montana, New Hampshire, Delaware, do not), and can be additionally layered by counties and cities within a state, meaning the *rate* and the *registration/permit regime* both vary below the country level — the correct unit of "who must register, and under what rules" in the US is the state (or even the local taxing jurisdiction), not the country. This is exactly the "sales tax nexus" concept: since *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.* (2018, not independently re-verified as part of this research but consistent with every source consulted), a business can owe sales tax collection obligations in a state purely from economic activity there (revenue/transaction-count thresholds), with no physical presence required — another way in which "country = tax jurisdiction" breaks down for the US specifically. One state was checked in detail as an illustrative example: **California** requires a seller's permit to make taxable sales (CDTFA), and California Revenue and Taxation Code **s.6071** makes it a misdemeanour to "engage in business as a seller in this state without a permit," punishable per s.7153 — this is the closest US analogue found to SA's s.58, but it criminalizes trading without a *permit*, not specifically the narrower act of showing a tax line on an invoice, and it is one state's law, not a national rule. **No other state's statute was checked** — a genuinely "US" entry in a country-keyed system would need per-state (and potentially per-locality) logic, not a single US-wide rule.

### 2.4 Australia — ATO / GST Act 1999

The GST Act 1999, s.29-70, defines the content requirements for a "tax invoice." ATO's own public guidance (ato.gov.au, "Tax invoices") states directly that a business not registered for GST "should not include the words 'tax invoice'" on its documents and must issue an ordinary invoice instead — structurally identical to SA's s.20 restriction on "tax invoice"/"VAT invoice" wording. (Direct WebFetch of the live ATO page returned HTTP 403 from this research environment; the wording above was confirmed via an indexed search excerpt of the same official ato.gov.au URL, cited above — flagged for transparency rather than treated as equivalent to a direct primary-source read.)

### 2.5 New Zealand — IRD / GST Act 1985

New Zealand changed its own terminology on **1 April 2023**: the long-standing "tax invoice" concept was formally replaced by a broader "taxable supply information" record-keeping obligation. IRD's own page confirms the obligation to provide this information falls on "the GST-registered person making the taxable supply" (the supplier) — so the registration-gating logic is unchanged in substance, only the label for the underlying legal concept changed. IRD explicitly notes that businesses do not need to stop using the words "tax invoice" on their documents for this to remain compliant — a business may continue to label a document "tax invoice" as a matter of convention even though the Act itself no longer uses that term as the operative legal concept. This is a useful data point for template design generally: legal terminology and conventional/UI terminology can diverge even within one jurisdiction, and IRD's own guidance treats that as fine.

### 2.6 Canada — CRA / Excise Tax Act

GST/HST registration obligations are set out in the Excise Tax Act. Every province combines the federal 5% GST with, in some cases, a harmonized provincial rate into a single HST rate (a genuinely more complex sub-national structure than a flat national rate, similar in kind — though not mechanism — to the US sales-tax fragmentation). Section 221(1) ties the obligation to collect tax to "every person who makes a taxable supply," acting as agent of the Crown; small suppliers below the CA$30,000 threshold are exempted from this obligation via a separate small-supplier provision rather than via a specific "unauthorised invoice" offence. **This research did not locate a Canadian statutory provision directly analogous to SA s.58/UK Schedule 41/India s.32/Australia's tax-invoice restriction** (i.e., a specific rule that an unregistered person must not show or charge tax on an invoice) within the time available — this is stated as an open gap, not as a finding that no such rule exists. It would need dedicated follow-up (a full-text search of the Excise Tax Act's offence provisions, ss. 326+) before any claim could be made either way.

### 2.7 India — CBIC / CGST Act 2017

India's GST is split into **CGST** (Central GST), **SGST** (State GST, for intra-state supplies — both charged together, each going to a different government), and **IGST** (Integrated GST, for inter-state and import supplies, collected by the Centre and apportioned). This split adds a dimension of complexity beyond a single "GST rate": which of CGST+SGST vs IGST applies depends on whether the *supplier's* and the *customer's* locations are in the same Indian state or different ones — a domestic-cross-state-border case that sits between "purely domestic" and "cross-border" in the international sense discussed in §1, but is resolved entirely within Indian law and does not involve a foreign country. **Section 32 of the CGST Act 2017** is the direct, on-point analogue to SA's restriction: *"A person who is not a registered person shall not collect in respect of any supply of goods or services or both any amount by way of tax under this Act"* — and separately, "no registered person shall collect tax except in accordance with the provisions of this Act." CBIC's own published guidance confirms non-payment of unlawfully collected tax is treated as an offence under s.122, with the collected-but-unpaid amount recoverable with interest under s.76.

### 2.8 A jurisdiction with no general consumption tax — Kuwait and Qatar

Within the Gulf Cooperation Council, all six member states signed the 2017 GCC VAT Framework Agreement, and four have since implemented VAT — UAE and Saudi Arabia (2018), Bahrain (2019), Oman (2021) — but **Kuwait and Qatar have not**, as of this research date (23 September 2026). This means Kuwait and Qatar currently have **no general consumption tax of any kind at the national level** — no VAT, no general sales tax — only narrow, specific excise taxes on selected goods (tobacco, sugary/energy drinks, and similar categories under discussion in Kuwait). This is a genuinely current example of the "no general consumption tax" category the research brief asked to identify.

**Source-reliability caveat, stated explicitly per this research's own standard**: unlike every other entry in this table, a country having *no* VAT/GST cannot be confirmed by pointing to "the statute that says so," because there is no VAT authority publishing "we have no VAT" as an official position — the absence itself is the fact being checked, and it is inherently harder to source to a single primary government document. No Kuwaiti or Qatari government (Ministry of Finance or equivalent tax-authority) page was successfully located and read directly as part of this research confirming the current non-implementation status; the finding rests on multiple independent tax-advisory/trade-press sources (not SARS-equivalent primary sources) that agree on the current status and on Kuwait's government having publicly ruled out VAT implementation before 2028. **This status should be treated as provisional** and re-verified against each country's own Ministry of Finance or National Assembly record before any code or documentation asserts it as settled fact — exactly the same caveat this repo's prior SA research applied to the unreachable SAICA/SAIT sources.

## 3. What data model changes this would imply (design-level, not code)

The codebase was inspected directly for this section (not researched externally) — `app/Models/Company.php`, `app/Models/Customer.php`, `app/Models/Address.php`, `app/Models/Country.php`, and the migrations that added `vat_id`/`tax_id` columns.

### 3.1 Current state — there is no country field on `Company` or `Customer` directly

Neither `Company` nor `Customer` has a `country` column of its own. Country only exists indirectly, via the `Address` model: `Company hasOne(Address::class)` and `Customer hasMany(Address::class)` (with `billingAddress()`/`shippingAddress()` scoped helpers), and `Address belongsTo(Country::class)` — `Country` being a simple reference table (`id`, `code`, `name`) used to look up a localized country name via Symfony's `Countries` component. So "the company's country" today is reachable only as `$company->address->country`, and "the customer's country" only as `$customer->billingAddress->country` (or `shippingAddress`) — both are **optional, nullable, address-level facts**, not a guaranteed, always-populated, first-class attribute of the company or customer record itself. An address (and therefore a country) can be entirely absent for either party today.

The existing SA-specific tax-gating logic (`app/Services/Document/InvoiceService.php`, confirmed by grep: `if ($invoice->company->vat_id)` / `if ($invoice->customer->vat_id || $invoice->customer->tax_id)`) does not reference country at all — it is a pure `vat_id`-presence check, which is only safe *because* this deployment is single-tenant and single-jurisdiction (SA-only) by assumption, not because the code itself enforces or checks that assumption anywhere.

### 3.2 What a country-aware design would need to add

1. **A reliable, non-optional "company's own tax jurisdiction" concept.** Today the company's country is an optional address attribute. A country-aware tax-label system needs this to be authoritative and always present for the company record — either promote it to a first-class, required field directly on `Company` (simplest), or keep it on `Address` but make the company's primary address (and its country) mandatory at the point a company is created/onboarded. Relying on an optional, nullable address relation as the source of truth for "which country's tax law governs this company" is fragile — a company with no address on file would have undefined tax-label behaviour.

2. **"Registered for VAT/GST/tax in country X" needs to be a distinct concept from "has an address in country X."** The research brief's own example is exactly right: a UK company can have an address in the UK, be UK-VAT-registered, and still deal with EU customers post-Brexit under different rules than an EU-resident company would face dealing with the same EU customers. Today, `vat_id` is a single free-text string on both `Company` and `Customer` (confirmed: `companies.vat_id`, `customers.vat_id`, both added via the migrations inspected) with **no field recording which country's tax authority issued that number**. A UK VAT number and a South African VAT number are both just strings in the same column today — there is nothing in the data model that says "this `vat_id` was issued by HMRC" vs "this `vat_id` was issued by SARS." For genuinely country-aware logic, the system would need, at minimum, a way to know *which* jurisdiction a given `vat_id` belongs to — either a paired `vat_country` field, or (more robust, since VAT number formats are largely country-specific — e.g. SA numbers are 10 digits, UK numbers are `GB` + 9 or 12 digits, EU numbers are a 2-letter country prefix + digits) validating/deriving the issuing country from the number format itself where the format is unambiguous, with an explicit fallback field where it isn't.

3. **A per-jurisdiction rule table, not per-jurisdiction hardcoded template logic.** The current template hardcodes the SA-specific rule (`$isVatRegistered` gate, "VAT" label) directly into the Blade templates and `InvoiceService.php`. A multi-country version would need this externalised into something keyed by jurisdiction — e.g. a small config/lookup structure per supported country recording: the tax name to display ("VAT", "GST", "Sales Tax"...), whether an unregistered issuer may show any tax-related row at all, what document title words are restricted ("tax invoice"/"VAT invoice" analogues, if any), and whether cross-border customer country ever changes the render (zero-rate note, reverse-charge note) per §1. This is a materially bigger change than the current single `if ($company->vat_id)` check — it is a rules-engine-shaped problem, not a single boolean.

4. **Customer country's role is narrower than company country's role**, per §1's finding — it should feed a cross-border flag/note (zero-rated export, reverse charge applies), not select its own independent label. The data model implication is smaller here: mainly, knowing reliably whether the customer's country differs from the company's country (which requires customer country to be reliably populated too, same caveat as §3.2.1), and — for reverse-charge scenarios specifically — potentially the customer's own VAT/GST number (already present as `customer.vat_id`) to state on the invoice, since EU-style reverse-charge invoices typically show the customer's VAT number as part of the "why no VAT was charged" justification.

## 4. Realistic scope recommendation

Full shipping-grade legal research (to the standard of the three prior SA-only research files, or §2's UK/EU/Australia entries) for every jurisdiction in the world is not feasible, and — per the "invent nothing" principle both this file and the prior SA research were built on — attempting to auto-generate confident legal claims for a country nobody actually checked is actively worse than not supporting that country at all. It would produce a template that looks authoritative for jurisdictions where the underlying legal claim was never verified.

**Recommended approach: a small, explicit allowlist of researched jurisdictions, with a safe, legally-inert fallback for everything else.**

1. **Allowlist scope**: start with South Africa (done, shipped) plus, only if and when TARCZA actually has or plans real business in that jurisdiction, one additional country researched to the same standard as the SA files before any code changes ship for it — not all eight jurisdictions surveyed in §2 at once. The countries researched in §2 above are a *starting menu* for future research rounds, not a batch of jurisdictions ready to implement; several (Canada, the EU as a bloc, the US) have explicitly-flagged open gaps in this research (§2.3, §2.6, §2.2) that would need closing before shipping logic for them specifically.

2. **Fallback behaviour for any country outside the allowlist**: **do not render a tax line at all, and do not use any jurisdiction-specific label.** This mirrors the prior research's own conclusion for the SA non-vendor case (§4 of `sa-vat-non-vendor-invoicing.md`): suppressing the row entirely carries no legal exposure, whereas guessing a label or a rule for an unresearched jurisdiction risks reproducing exactly the mislabeling/overclaiming problem this whole line of research exists to prevent. A generic, unconditional "Tax" label as the fallback (rather than no row at all) was considered and rejected for the same reason `vat-vs-tax-invoice-terminology.md` gives: a bare "Tax" label doesn't actually disambiguate anything for an unresearched jurisdiction — it just looks confident without being backed by anything. No row is the honest fallback.

3. **Trigger for adding a jurisdiction to the allowlist**: a real, current or firmly planned TARCZA customer or company registration in that country — not speculative "might expand there someday" scope creep. Each addition should be its own dedicated research round, held to the same primary-source bar as this file and the three SA files, before any template code changes for that jurisdiction.

## Verdict

**(a) Direct answer.** Yes, invoice tax terminology *should* eventually become country-aware if TARCZA ever operates in, or bills customers in, more than one jurisdiction — but the driver should overwhelmingly be the **issuing company's own registered country and registration status**, not the customer's country. §1 established this is how VAT/GST systems work almost everywhere: origin/supplier-registration-based for domestic sales, with the customer's country only entering the picture for genuinely cross-border supplies, and even then only as a zero-rate/reverse-charge flag or note — never as an independent selector of its own tax label. A design that treats "customer's country" as equally weighted with "company's country" in picking the displayed label would be modelling the mechanism incorrectly for every jurisdiction checked.

**(b) No code should change yet.** This document is a design/legal research foundation only. The current South-Africa-only `vat_id`-gated logic (Blade templates + `InvoiceService.php`, as described in `docs/typst/sa-vat-invoicing-research.typ`'s "Code changes implemented" section) is untouched and should stay exactly as it is — TARCZA's real South African invoices continue to be generated by the existing, already-verified logic. Everything in this file is for the developer to review before deciding whether, when, and how to act on it.

**(c) Recommended jurisdiction scope and fallback.** Start with South Africa only (already shipped). Add a second jurisdiction only when TARCZA has real, current business need there, researched to the SA files' standard before any code ships. For every country outside the researched allowlist: **render no tax line at all, rather than a generic "Tax" label or a guessed rule** — this is the only fallback with zero risk of reproducing a legal claim nobody actually checked.

**(d) Further work needed before shipping this for any non-SA jurisdiction.** At minimum: (i) close the explicitly-flagged gaps in this research — Canada's offence-provision search was not exhaustive, the EU entry deliberately did not check any individual member state's national transposition, and the US entry checked exactly one state (California) out of dozens with a sales tax; (ii) the data-model changes in §3 (a reliable non-optional company/customer country field, and a way to know which country issued a given `vat_id`) would need to be designed and built before country-aware logic has anything real to key off; (iii) **and, regardless of how thorough this document's own research is, an internationally-qualified tax advisor's sign-off is required before shipping tax-display logic for any jurisdiction outside South Africa** — this document, like the three SA research files it extends, is developer-level legal research intended to inform a decision, not a substitute for professional advice, and that is especially true here: SA's three prior files had the benefit of a single, deeply-researched statute; this file necessarily spreads much thinner across eight jurisdictions and explicitly flags several claims as unconfirmed or partially confirmed. Nothing in this document should be relied on to make an actual invoicing decision for a non-South-African entity without a locally-qualified tax practitioner (or, for South Africa specifically, a registered SA tax practitioner as the three prior files already state) reviewing it first.
