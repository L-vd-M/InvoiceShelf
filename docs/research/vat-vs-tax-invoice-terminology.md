# "VAT" vs "Tax" on an Invoice — Conceptual Explainer

Research date: 2026-09-23. Companion/prerequisite reading (both in this folder, read first — this file cross-references rather than repeats them):

- `docs/research/sa-vat-non-vendor-invoicing.md` — established that VAT Act s.58 makes it a criminal offence for a non-VAT-vendor to declare/charge tax, and that s.20 restricts the term "tax invoice"/"VAT invoice" to registered vendors.
- `docs/research/sa-invoice-tax-vs-vat-labeling.md` — established that VAT Act s.1 defines `"tax" means the tax chargeable under this Act` (i.e. "tax" is legally a synonym for VAT inside the Act itself, since VAT is the only tax the Act charges), and that SARS's own VAT 404 Guide for Vendors labels real rand amounts "VAT" in its plain-English worked examples, never generic "Tax".

## 1. The general/international accounting distinction

"VAT" and "Tax" are not two names for the same thing at the conceptual level — they sit at different levels of abstraction, and conflating them is the actual root of why invoicing software gets this wrong.

**"VAT" (Value-Added Tax) is a specific, named mechanism**: a multi-stage consumption tax charged at *every* step of a supply chain — manufacturer to distributor, distributor to retailer, retailer to consumer — where each VAT-registered business in the chain charges VAT on its sales ("output tax"), reclaims the VAT it paid on its own purchases ("input tax"), and remits only the *difference* to the tax authority. The mechanism is designed so the tax never compounds: only the final, non-registered consumer at the end of the chain bears the full cost, because only they cannot reclaim anything. VAT (or its Commonwealth-flavoured sibling GST — Goods and Services Tax, used in Australia, Canada, India, New Zealand, and functionally identical in mechanism, differing mainly in terminology and scope) is used by more than 170 countries, including every EU member state, the UK, and South Africa (Quaderno; BDO — general-mechanism sources only, not relied on for any SA-specific legal claim).

**US-style sales tax is a structurally different animal**, not a rebranding of the same idea: it is a *single-stage* tax charged only once, at the final retail sale to the end consumer. A manufacturer selling to a distributor, or a distributor selling to a retailer, charges no sales tax at all on that intermediate sale (typically backed by a resale exemption certificate) — the tax appears exactly once, at the register, on the transaction with the end customer. There is no input-tax-credit mechanism, because there is nothing to credit: businesses further up the chain never paid the tax in the first place. Rates and rules also vary by state, county, and even city in the US, so "sales tax" is never one fixed number the way SA's 15% VAT rate is (BDO; Fonoa).

**"Tax" as a generic invoice-software field label exists because of this exact fragmentation.** A piece of invoicing software built to serve customers in many countries — or, very concretely, software originally built for the US market (where "Sales Tax" is the locally correct term but doesn't travel) and later adapted for international use — cannot hardcode a jurisdiction-specific noun into its UI. It cannot show "Sales Tax" to a South African customer (wrong mechanism, wrong term) or "VAT" to a Texan customer (Texas has no VAT). So the software reaches for the most neutral possible word — "Tax" — as a placeholder that can be *relabeled per-locale* or left generic when the target jurisdiction isn't known. This is a real, defensible design decision **in a multi-jurisdiction product**. It stops being a defensible decision the moment the product (or a fork of it, or one deployment/tenant of it) is scoped to a single jurisdiction that has exactly one such tax — at that point the generic label is no longer doing disambiguation work, because there's nothing left to disambiguate (see §4).

## 2. South Africa specifically: is there ever a "Tax" on a SA invoice that isn't VAT?

The prior research (`sa-invoice-tax-vs-vat-labeling.md` §3) already established that VAT is South Africa's only transaction-based consumption tax ever itemised on an ordinary trading/services invoice, and ruled out Turnover Tax, excise/environmental levies, and withholding tax on the grounds that none of them are passed through line-by-line to a customer. This section checks that finding against SARS's own primary pages for every plausible SA tax/levy type, one at a time, to confirm none of them is ever an invoice-to-customer line item.

| Tax/levy | Who pays it, to whom | Ever a line on a sales invoice TO A CUSTOMER? | SARS source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PAYE** (employees' tax) | Employer withholds from an **employee's** salary/wages, remits to SARS monthly via EMP201 | **No.** SARS's own definition: *"Employees' Tax refers to the tax required to be deducted by an employer from an employee's remuneration paid or payable."* This is strictly an employer↔employee payroll relationship. There is no "customer" in this transaction at all — an employee is not a customer, and a payslip is not an invoice. | https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/pay-as-you-earn/ |
| **Withholding Tax on Royalties** (non-residents) | The SA payer withholds from a **royalty payment made to a non-resident**, remits to SARS via the WTR01 return | **No, and the direction is backwards from an invoice anyway.** SARS: *"The foreign person is liable for the tax, but the tax must be withheld from the royalty payment by the person paying it to the foreign person (i.e. the withholding agent)."* This is a deduction the SA business makes from a payment it is *making out* (to a non-resident IP owner), not an amount it *adds onto* a sales invoice it is issuing to a customer. It would appear, if anywhere, on a payment/remittance advice to the non-resident payee — never on a customer-facing sales invoice. | https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/withholding-tax-on-royalties/ |
| **Withholding Tax on Interest/Dividends** (non-residents) | Same mechanism as royalties — payer withholds from payment made to non-resident, remits to SARS | **No**, same reasoning as above — payment-out mechanism, not a customer-invoice line. Dividends Tax is 20% (subject to treaty reduction), interest withholding 15% (with exemptions), both final taxes on the non-resident recipient, both collected by the SA payer withholding from its own outgoing payment. | https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/dividends-tax/, https://www.sars.gov.za/tax-rates/income-tax/interest-and-dividends/ |
| **Skills Development Levy (SDL)** | Employer pays 1% of its total payroll to SARS via EMP201, where payroll exceeds R500,000/year | **No.** This is a levy calculated on the *employer's own salary bill*, not on any sale. It has no relationship to a customer transaction and is never itemised on a sales invoice — it's a payroll-driven cost the business simply absorbs like any other overhead. | https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/skills-development-levy/ |
| **UIF (Unemployment Insurance Fund) contributions** | 2% of remuneration (1% employee-withheld + 1% employer-matched), paid to SARS via EMP201, same declaration as PAYE/SDL | **No**, same reasoning as PAYE — an employer↔employee payroll deduction, not a customer-invoice item. | https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/unemployment-insurance-fund/ |
| **Customs Duty / Excise Duty** | Importer pays customs duty at the border on imported goods (Customs and Excise Act 91 of 1964); manufacturers pay excise duty on specified locally-manufactured goods and their imported equivalents (e.g. alcohol, tobacco, fuel, and ad valorem excise on luxury goods like perfumes, firearms, arcade games) | **Generally no for an ordinary reseller — with one genuine exception.** For a business that resells imported/excisable goods domestically, customs/excise duty is folded into the landed cost price before the goods are even offered for sale — it is a cost input, not a separately-quoted line, exactly as the prior research found for excise/environmental levies. **The one real-world exception**: a customs clearing agent or freight forwarder that pays duty *on a client's behalf* as a disbursement will itemise "Customs Duty paid" as a distinct line on *their own* invoice to that client — but that is a disbursement recovery (a pass-through cost, not a "tax I am charging you"), issued by a specialised clearing-agent business, not an ordinary trading/services invoice. TARCZA is not a customs clearing agent, so this exception doesn't apply, but it's worth knowing the exception exists rather than stating an absolute "never." | https://www.sars.gov.za/customs-and-excise/, https://www.sars.gov.za/customs-and-excise/duties-and-taxes/, https://www.sars.gov.za/customs-and-excise/excise/ |
| **Turnover Tax** | Qualifying micro-business (turnover ≤ R2.3m/year, per the current threshold) pays 0–3% of its *own* turnover directly to SARS | **No.** It's a simplified substitute for income tax, paid by the business on its own revenue — it is never passed through to, or itemised on, a customer's invoice. Confirms the prior research's finding. | https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/turnover-tax/ |
| **Provisional Tax / Income Tax** | The business or individual pays estimated income tax in advance, directly to SARS, based on the business's own taxable income | **No — not even conceptually adjacent to an invoice.** SARS is explicit that *"Provisional tax is not a separate tax from income tax. It is a method of paying the income tax liability in advance."* It has no transactional trigger at all (no "sale" event causes it) and no mechanism by which it could ever appear on a document issued to a customer. | https://www.sars.gov.za/guide-to-provisional-tax/, https://www.sars.gov.za/types-of-tax/provisional-tax/ |

**Conclusion:** every one of the plausible "other SA tax" candidates is either (a) a payroll deduction between an employer and its own employee, (b) a withholding deducted from a payment the business makes *outward* to someone else, (c) a duty/levy absorbed into cost price before the sale rather than itemised at the point of sale, or (d) a tax the business pays on its own turnover/income with no per-transaction trigger at all. **None of them is ever a line item added onto a sales invoice issued to a customer.** VAT remains, confirmed exhaustively rather than just asserted, the only South African tax that is ever added as a percentage-of-sale-value line on an outgoing customer invoice.

## 3. Statutory drafting language vs public-facing label — why the gap exists

The prior research (`sa-invoice-tax-vs-vat-labeling.md` §1–§2) already found the specific facts: the VAT Act's own operative sections use the bare word "tax" (s.1 defines it, s.20(4)(g)(i) requires "the amount of tax charged"), while SARS's own plain-English VAT 404 Guide for Vendors uses "VAT" for every real rand amount in its worked examples. This section explains *why* that gap exists and what a developer should do with it.

**Why the Act uses "tax":** this is a standard legislative drafting convention, not an inconsistency or an oversight. A statute defines a term once, precisely, in its definitions section (VAT Act s.1: `"tax" means the tax chargeable under this Act`), and then uses that short defined term throughout the rest of the document instead of repeating the full concept every time. This keeps the operative text tight and internally consistent — every appearance of "tax" in the Act means exactly the s.1 definition, no more and no less, and a reader working through the statute is expected to hold that definition in mind. It is the same reason contracts define "the Agreement," "the Parties," or "the Effective Date" once and then use the short form for the rest of the document. The defined term is an internal cross-reference device for people reading the *statute itself*, not a communication choice aimed at ordinary vendors or customers.

**Why SARS's public guidance uses "VAT":** the VAT 404 Guide for Vendors is explicitly a plain-English operational guide, not a legal reference (its own Preface says so, per the prior research). Its audience is a vendor working out how much VAT to charge, or a bookkeeper reconciling input and output tax — people who need to recognise the tax by its actual, specific, commonly-used name, not by an internal statutory shorthand they'd have to already know maps back to "VAT" via s.1. Public communication optimises for immediate recognisability: "VAT" is the term every South African vendor, customer, bank statement, and receipt already uses and understands. Using the Act's internal shorthand ("tax") in a document meant for the public would force the reader to do a lookup ("which tax? oh right, s.1 says 'tax' means VAT") that serves no purpose for them — they already know which tax it is, because in South Africa there's only one.

**Practical implication for someone designing a UI or invoice template:** these are two different registers of the same underlying fact, aimed at two different readers. A statute's internal shorthand is written for lawyers and drafters cross-referencing the Act against itself. A customer-facing invoice is written for a human who wants to know, at a glance, what the extra amount on their bill is. **Match SARS's public-facing convention ("VAT") when writing for actual humans/customers — not the Act's internal drafting shorthand ("tax").** The Act's use of "tax" being technically legal for a registered vendor (per the prior research's §1) is a fact about statutory compliance, not a style recommendation; SARS itself, writing for the public, doesn't follow its own Act's internal shorthand, and neither should an invoice template.

## 4. Practical rule of thumb for an invoicing system

Pulling §1–§3 together into one mental model for a developer:

**A generic "Tax" field label is a solution to a problem that exists only in multi-jurisdiction software.** It exists because a product sold into many countries cannot hardcode "VAT" for a South African customer and "Sales Tax" for a Texan one into the same UI string — so it picks a neutral placeholder word that can, in principle, mean whatever the local consumption tax happens to be. That is a legitimate design compromise *when the jurisdiction is unknown or variable*.

**South Africa is not a jurisdiction where that ambiguity exists.** Per §2 above (now checked exhaustively against every plausible candidate tax, not just asserted), South Africa has exactly one transaction-based consumption tax that is ever itemised on a sales invoice: VAT. There is no second tax that a generic "Tax" line could be quietly standing in for — no VAT-plus-local-sales-tax stacking the way some countries have, no per-province/state variation the way the US does. If an invoice generated by a South Africa-only system ever shows an amount that is a percentage of the sale value (or any fixed VAT-type charge), that amount is, by definition of the SA tax landscape, VAT — full stop.

**The rule of thumb:** in a South Africa-only system, there is no ambiguity left for a generic "Tax" label to resolve, so a generic "Tax" field is not a real reflection of any actual choice a South African invoice could need to make — it is a leftover artifact of jurisdiction-agnostic software design (see §1: this fork's presumable upstream, like most invoicing software, was built to serve many countries or was US-market-first). The label should say "VAT," because "VAT" is the only thing it could ever correctly say once you already know you're in South Africa.

## Verdict

**(a) Direct answer.** "VAT" is a specific tax mechanism (multi-stage, input-tax-credit-based, currently 15% in South Africa). "Tax" is a generic umbrella label that invoicing software uses to avoid hardcoding a jurisdiction-specific term, so the same UI can serve countries with VAT, GST, or US-style single-stage sales tax without rewriting the label per market. In South Africa specifically, that ambiguity collapses to zero: VAT is the *only* transaction-based consumption tax ever itemised on a sales invoice to a customer (confirmed in §2 against PAYE, withholding tax on royalties/interest/dividends, SDL, UIF, customs/excise duty, Turnover Tax, and provisional/income tax — none of them is ever a customer-invoice line item), so a "Tax" amount on a South African invoice is definitionally a VAT amount, and the label should say so.

**(b) Does this change anything about the TARCZA invoice template as currently built?** **No — this is confirmatory, not a further code change.** The prior two research rounds already produced the operative rules and (per this session's context) the corresponding code changes: suppress the tax row entirely when `company.vat_id` is empty (from `sa-vat-non-vendor-invoicing.md`), and label the row "VAT" rather than generic "Tax" when `company.vat_id` is populated (from `sa-invoice-tax-vs-vat-labeling.md`). This explainer doesn't surface a new SA tax type that was missed, doesn't find an exception to the "VAT is the only line-item consumption tax" finding for TARCZA's actual business (ordinary trading/services, not customs clearing), and doesn't disturb the `vat_id`-gated logic already established. It exists to document *why* that logic is correct at a conceptual level — useful for onboarding, for a future contributor questioning "why does this fork say VAT instead of Tax", and for reuse if TARCZA or a sibling project ever targets a second jurisdiction — not to change the template again.

**(c) What would flip this verdict:**

- **If South Africa ever introduced a second transaction-based consumption tax** stacked alongside VAT (e.g. a separate luxury/digital-services transaction tax charged at the point of sale, distinct from VAT) — at that point a bare "Tax" label would become genuinely ambiguous in the SA context for the first time, and the correct fix would be a specific, named second line (not a reversion to a generic "Tax" catch-all).
- **If TARCZA ever became a customs clearing agent, freight forwarder, or otherwise started itemising customs/excise duty as a disbursement line on invoices to clients** (the one genuine exception identified in §2's table) — that would introduce a second, legitimately-separate line item that is a real duty amount, not VAT, and would need its own distinct label (e.g. "Customs Duty (disbursement)"), not a merge into the VAT row and not a reversion to generic "Tax" either.
- **If TARCZA expanded to serve customers or entities in a second country** (even just cross-border invoicing to a non-SA customer) — at that point the single-jurisdiction assumption underlying §4's whole argument no longer holds, and a genuinely jurisdiction-aware label (or a per-invoice/per-entity locale setting) would become necessary again, closer to how the upstream multi-country product was originally designed.

This is not a substitute for a registered tax practitioner's sign-off — get one before relying on this document for a live invoicing decision.
